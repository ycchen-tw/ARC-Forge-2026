# LP85 — Rotating shared rings onto targets
Keywords: ring rotation, cyclic permutation, shared cells, click buttons, one button drives several cycles, parking slot, wraparound jump, color-matched targets, modular parity lock, prefix sub-cycle, token tracking, inverse button pairs
Looks like: A dark board with a few loops or lines of small colored squares and arrow-shaped buttons at the edges. The only action is a click at a coordinate, and only arrow clicks do anything. Colored corner brackets mark target cells, and a bar at the left edge counts down the budget.

## Rules
- Each ring is a cyclic list of cell positions. One click on a ring's button moves every block on it one step along the cycle, and an empty cell travels with the rest. Buttons come in two colors, one meaning "step back" and one "step forward". Some rings have only one direction, so you must wrap around to go back.
- Consecutive ring positions need not be adjacent on screen. Straight lines and separate corners are closed by jumps, and some rings advance several cells per step.
- Rings overlap at shared cells. A block sitting on a shared cell moves with whichever ring you rotate, which is the only way to transfer blocks between rings.
- One button can rotate several rings at once, because stacked button sprites all fire. The rings in a group never share cells. Many buttons are decorative copies of the same effect.
- Win: every bracket frame holds a block of its own color, and blocks of the same color are interchangeable. Swapping two different colors does not win. The winning click is free.
- Only clicks on a button cost budget (one per click, however many rings it drives). Clicks on blocks or empty space are free no-ops. A level change resets the board, and there is no undo.
- Some blocks are decorations on no ring. The same colored blocks that are targets exist in the exact number of brackets.

## Levels
- L1: One 20-cell loop with a back and a forward button. Count the steps from the block to the bracket.
- L2: A loop plus two straight lines, each with its own button pair. Each line shares two cells with the loop, and the line wraps end to end. Two brackets.
- L3: Two 16-cell loops sharing two cells. The brackets have different colors, so each block must be carried through a shared cell into the other loop.
- L4: Four corner clusters with 16 buttons, but only two 20-cycles with crosswise jumps. The four centres are shared.
- L5: A 5-cell top row is part of a winding 21-cell loop. Two same-color brackets sit at the row's ends.
- L6: Three 8-spoke clusters (36 rings in all) plus one far-off button, and every button is forward-only. One button per cluster shifts all the spokes outward. Another rotates every ring. The odd button swaps each bracket with its cluster's tip. (hard: cleared 13/13, median 34 min)
- L7: A row of 8 and a cluster of 4 turn together from one button pair. A 3-cell column shares one cell with the row. The column holds the ring that needs the shared-cell trick.
- L8: Three long diagonal-then-column cycles all turn from one bottom button pair. Three side button pairs rotate only short prefixes of those cycles. (hard: cleared 13/13, median 23 min)

## Blind spots — where our player lost time
1. **Reading a cycle from one click (L8, also L7).**
 **Believed:** one click per button is enough to recover each cycle's order.
 **Actually:** many blocks share colors, so one click is consistent with many permutations. Probing ate 18 to 47 actions where the optimum is 5 (9 copies).
 **Cue:** the diff shows a two-cell change, or the wrap point looks the same in both directions.
 **Test:** click the same button 2 to 3 times and require each block's color to match at every step. Or make the colors distinct first.
2. **Odd button in L6 modeled as an ordinary rotation (3 copies).**
 **Believed:** each arrow has an inverse partner, or the odd arrow is a 4-cycle or ring swap.
 **Actually:** the far arrow is an involution of three independent transpositions. It is the only button touching the brackets, and a bracket is not sticky. A second click pulls the feeder cell's old content back in.
 **Cue:** all arrows are the same color, and there are no inverse pairs.
 **Test:** click it twice and check the board returns. Then make the two candidate cells differ in color and click again.
3. **Locked coupling in L7: row and cluster read as one cycle, and the column as a swap (4 copies).**
 **Believed:** the 8-ring and 4-ring were a single 12-cycle, or the 3-cell column was a 2-cell swap.
 **Actually:** two disjoint cycles rotate together under one button, and the column is a 3-cycle.
 **Cue:** first probes matched because colors repeated.
 **Test:** diff a state with all-distinct values at the suspected wrap before planning.
4. **Same-color targets treated as exchangeable (L3, 4 copies).**
 **Believed:** any pair of colored blocks fills any pair of brackets, or a direction sign guessed by analogy would work.
 **Actually:** each bracket needs its own color. Swapped assignments don't clear, and the direction was opposite to the guess.
 **Cue:** the brackets have different colors.
 **Test:** replay one recorded click through the model before planning.
5. **L4 cross layout treated as independent clusters (4 copies).**
 **Believed:** each row or column is its own 10-cycle, or each cluster is independent.
 **Actually:** every button of a kind drives the same two 20-cycles, with crosswise wraps.
 **Cue:** 16 buttons but all clicks of one type move blocks in distant clusters.
 **Test:** click one arrow and see whether far-away blocks move.

## Breakthroughs
- Shared cells are the transfer route, and sometimes the lever that breaks a lock between cycles (L2, L5, L7). A block parked on a shared cell is moved by a different button pair while the other rings turn.
- Only the goal-colored blocks matter, so search over their positions rather than the full board (L3). Full-board search ran out of memory (L3, L6).
- In L6, park each block on its own feeder cell, then press the swap button once. In L8, a master button plus short prefix rotations breaks the parity lock. Put each block at the start of its prefix, then use one master step to wrap all three onto their brackets.
- Diagnose with distinct colors and repeated application, then verify by replaying the recorded history in code.

## Lessons that carry to other games
- If one control moves several groups with different periods, check whether the combined periods are even compatible with the goal before searching.
- A first-pass model that fits one or two probes may only match because of repeated colors, so test it on a state with distinct values.
- Look for any cell touched by two controls. It is the place to hold something while the other group turns.
- When goals can be reached only by one special control, plan backward from it and make its preconditions true all at once.
- Assume nothing about direction, and don't assume pairs of controls are inverses until a click and its partner restore the board.
